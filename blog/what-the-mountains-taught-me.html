What the Mountains Taught Me About Surviving Film Production

By Shane Foster · October 15, 2026

The Easter eggs were hidden in holes in the snow. It was April, and there were four feet of snow on the ground. I was probably nine or ten years old. My mom put the eggs down into the snow, and we went out in our pajamas, barefoot, and hunted for them.

Nobody in that house thought an Easter egg hunt in four feet of snow was strange. That was just home. We lived near Wishon Lake, at about 7,000 foot elevation, where it snowed nine months out of the year. In a place like that, cold is not an event that shows up once a winter. Cold is most of the year. So I was a kid who walked around barefoot in the snow and wore shorts in it. I was used to really cold weather.

The Day I Almost Lost My Toes

Used to it did not mean safe from it. One day I was out riding my four wheeler through the backcountry, in tennis shoes. I flipped the bike. I could not get it started again. So I walked home. It was a few miles. In the snow. In tennis shoes. That is the day I almost lost my toes to frostbite.

The flip was the accident. The walk was the job. There was no other way home, so home was however far my own feet could take me. A kid learns something on a walk like that, and he does not learn it from a book: when nobody is coming to fix it, you fix it by moving.

Survival Was Not a Hobby

That is why survival was not a hobby up there. It was not something you read about for fun and put back on the shelf. You thought about it a lot, because the place you lived in made you think about it. My dad taught me a great deal, especially about hunting and survival, and my brother taught me too. I read books, and I experimented on my own. And the rest of the schooling was play, though we would not have called it that. We played war, and we pretended to be ninjas. We built booby traps and trip lines that would drop pinecones on you. It was fun. It was also practice in paying attention to the ground in front of you: what is strung where, what gives way, what falls.

Fifty-Two Miles to School

The mountains gave a big lesson now and then, but they gave small ones every day, and the school run was the daily one. High school was 52 miles away. One way. In the winter, the drive took two hours. I got carsick on the windy curves, so I learned to count the snow poles going by to keep from getting sick. I could not eat breakfast before that drive, so I ate at school, when I got there.

Counting poles sounds like nothing. It was the whole trick. You did not fight the whole two hours at once. You took one pole, then the next one. A long, hard thing gets done in small pieces, one piece at a time. I have used that more times since than I can count. I just stopped using poles.

Post Holes and Film Sets

Years later I found myself on film sets, and the ground there felt familiar. I think every film set pushes you to your limits in one way or another, and you have to have that mental toughness to stick it out. There have been times where I lost it on set, because the pressure was too much and I was frustrated with the people around me. But I stuck it out, and I got it done.

Part of why I can stick it out is post holes. As a kid, I helped my family build a log fence for our horses, and I dug post holes with a post hole digger, through decomposed granite. It was not one hole. It was as many holes as I could get done in four to six hours, all 92 pounds of me fighting that granite, three feet down, one hole after another. Day after day. Now that is persistence. And blisters too. Granite does not negotiate. It does not care how old you are, and the holes do not get shallower because your hands hurt. You dig until the fence can stand. A film set is not so different. The day does not get shorter because it is going badly. You dig until the work can stand.

What Nature's Trail Is About

All of that is in the film I have been writing for four years. Nature's Trail comes out of living in those mountains, and every draft of it has been written in ScriptForge. That is part of why ScriptForge exists at all: I built the tool because this film needed a better one. What I am going for in this film is the beauty of nature, and respect for it. It shows a little bit of God in nature. And if you do not respect it, you face its wrath.

I do not say that as a man guessing at a theme. I say it as the kid in the tennis shoes, walking home through the snow. As the kid counting snow poles on the way to school. As the kid with blisters from a post hole digger. The mountains taught me the beauty first, and the respect right behind it, and this film is my way of handing both of them on.

Respect. That is what I want the film to be about. That is what I want people to see: respect is the key to healing our society.
